What NEC 220.87 Actually Requires

The existing-load calculation in plain English — what the code asks for, what it leaves open, and where crews most often get it wrong.

Updated Aug 24, 2026

Most residential service upgrades start the same way. A homeowner wants a heat pump or an EV charger, someone runs a standard load calculation against a 100-amp service, the number comes back over capacity, and the quote grows by several thousand dollars.

Often the panel was fine.

A standard load calculation is a worst-case model. It assumes a house that runs everything at once, forever. NEC 220.87 offers a different route for services that already exist: instead of modeling what the building might draw, measure what it actually has drawn, and build from there.

The core idea

220.87 lets you establish existing load from the utility's own metered maximum demand. That number is not an estimate — it is what the meter recorded, in the real building, through a real winter.

From there the adequacy test is straightforward:

1.25 × (existing measured peak) + 100% of concurrent new load ≤ service capacity

Three pieces. Each one has a detail worth knowing.

Piece one: the existing measured peak

This comes from the utility, not from a clamp meter and not from a calculation.

The code is written against a 15-minute demand interval. If your utility supplies hourly data, it has to be converted before use, and the report should disclose what interval the source data actually used. Coarser intervals smooth away short peaks, and a peak that gets smoothed away is capacity you think you have but do not.

There are two qualifying routes:

  • Condition (1) — maximum demand data covering a 12-month period. This is the normal path and the one most utilities can serve directly.
  • The 30-day Exception — where a full year is unavailable, a 30-day recording made under specified conditions. This route carries restrictions that Condition (1) does not, including restrictions tied to on-site generation and load management.

Those restrictions are frequently misapplied to Condition (1) work where they simply do not belong. If you have twelve months of data, you are on the main path.

Piece two: the 125% factor

The 1.25 multiplier attaches to the existing measured demand only. It is the code's headroom allowance against the possibility that the measured year was not the worst year.

It does not attach to the new load. See the next piece — this is the single most argued-about point in the section.

Piece three: new load at 100%

New load enters the calculation at 100% of nameplate or MCA, with no demand factor applied.

This surprises people who are used to applying demand factors elsewhere in Article 220. The reasoning: 220.87 says "125%" explicitly where it means it, and says nothing at all about derating new load. Other sections of the code state "125% + 100%" outright when that is the intent. 220.87 does not.

The section is genuinely silent on the question, and that silence is debated in the trade rather than settled. Taking new load at full nameplate is the conservative reading — it is the interpretation that cannot be attacked for understating the result, which is exactly the property you want in something an inspector is going to review.

The filter on new load is not magnitude. It is concurrency.

Concurrency: what actually counts against you

A load that cannot physically run at the same time as the existing peak does not add to the existing peak.

The classic case is a heat pump replacing electric resistance heat, or an EV charger on a schedule that runs overnight when the building's measured peak occurs at 6 p.m. on a January evening. If the new load genuinely cannot coincide with the peak, it is excluded from the adequacy math — though it should still be disclosed in the report so the reviewer can see the whole picture and reach their own conclusion.

One important caution: concurrency reasoning draws on the logic of NEC 220.60, but it is not a codified step of 220.87. Any report should say so plainly rather than implying the code prescribes the determination. An AHJ who reads a report claiming code authority it does not have will discount the whole document — and they would be right to.

The mistake that costs the most: three-phase capacity

Service capacity is the denominator of the whole exercise. Getting it wrong invalidates everything above it.

  • Single-phase: capacity = volts × amps
  • Three-phase: capacity = √3 × volts × amps

Miss the √3 on a three-phase service and you understate capacity by roughly 42%. That produces a false failure — a recommendation to upgrade a service that never needed upgrading.

This is a costly error in a specific and unhelpful direction. It never puts anyone in danger, so nothing about the result looks alarming; it just quietly bills a customer for several thousand dollars of unnecessary work.

Watch 208V especially. It is genuinely ambiguous: 120/208V exists as a real single-phase configuration as well as the common three-phase one. The voltage alone does not tell you the phase. Confirm the service configuration rather than inferring it from the number.

Behind-the-meter solar changes what the meter means

If the building has PV, the utility's export may be net consumption — what the building pulled from the grid after on-site generation was subtracted.

Net data understates true consumption, sometimes dramatically. A house that drew 12 kW while its array produced 5 kW may show as 7 kW on a net export. Build a capacity argument on that number and you have understated the real load by 40%.

What matters is what the export actually contains, not what the utility is assumed to provide. Some utilities' standard exports are directional rather than gross. If gross consumption is recoverable from the data, use it. If only net is available, you can still proceed — but the report must say so, so the reviewer can weigh it.

What this section does not do

220.87 establishes existing load. It does not:

  • Approve anything. Only the AHJ does that.
  • Replace engineering judgment on the rest of the installation.
  • Guarantee any particular jurisdiction will accept the approach, though many do.

Where TrueLoad fits
