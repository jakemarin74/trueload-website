NEC 220.87 Explained: Load Calculations From Measured Demand

A plain-language guide to NEC 220.87, the code section that lets an existing service load be based on what the meter actually recorded. What it requires, how the 125% rule works, and when it beats a traditional load calculation.

Updated Sep 21, 2026  ·  Download the printable PDF

NEC 220.87 is the section of the National Electrical Code that lets a load calculation for an existing service or feeder be based on actual measured maximum demand, instead of nameplate ratings and generalized demand factors.

In short: it is based on how a building actually performs, not on a modeled, worst-case assumption. For a contractor adding a heat pump, EV charger or water heater to an older home, it is often the difference between "the panel is fine" and "you need a service upgrade first."

What 220.87 requires

The section is short. Paraphrased, the existing load may be taken from measured demand when all of the following are true:

  1. Maximum demand data is available for a one-year period. Twelve months captures both the heating and the cooling season, which is why utility smart-meter (AMI) interval data is such a good fit.
  2. The measured maximum demand at 125 percent, plus the new load, does not exceed the rating of the service (or the ampacity of the feeder).
  3. The service has overload protection and the feeder has overcurrent protection as the code already requires elsewhere.

There is an exception for buildings with no year of data: demand can be recorded for a minimum of 30 days with a recording ammeter or power meter, provided the recording reflects the building while occupied and accounts for the larger of the heating or cooling load and other seasonal loads. Recent code editions do not allow that 30-day exception where the service has on-site renewable generation or uses peak-load shaving, because the meter would not see the true load.

"Maximum demand" here means the highest average power sustained over a 15-minute interval, which is why 15-minute interval data is the ideal input.

220.87 is explicitly permitted by the NEC. It is not a workaround. It asks for good data and clear documentation, and the Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) always retains full authority to decide whether the method was applied appropriately.

The code text itself is free to read on NFPA's free-access site. Always work from the edition your jurisdiction has adopted.

The arithmetic, with an example

The whole calculation is three lines:

Step Example
Measured 15-minute peak over 12 months 9.7 kW, which is about 40 A at 240 V
Multiply by 125% 50 A
Add the new load 50 A + 32 A heat pump = 82 A
Compare with the service rating 82 A is less than 100 A, so the service is adequate

The 125 percent factor is the code's built-in safety margin. It is part of the method, not something you add on top.

Two ways to calculate load

Traditional calculations size a service using standardized tables built around an average home from decades ago. 220.87 instead asks what this specific home actually draws, using the utility meter already on the wall.

Traditional load calculation NEC 220.87
What it's based on Nameplate ratings and standardized demand factors Actual measured demand from the utility meter
What it reflects A modeled, worst-case estimate How the building actually performs
Where the data comes from Manufacturer specs and code tables 12 months of utility interval (AMI / smart meter) data
Typical result Often overstates real-world demand Reflects the true measured peak, almost always lower

For a closer comparison with the optional method for existing dwellings, see NEC 220.83 vs. 220.87.

The research behind it

Two Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) studies form the evidence base:

  • Less & Walker, Applied Energy 410:127422
  • Murphy et al., Journal of Building Engineering 120:115576

Across 11,940 homes, the mean measured 15-minute peak demand was just 9.7 kW. A standard 100-amp, 240-volt service delivers 24 kW. On average, 220.87 produces roughly 40% lower calculated loads than the 220.83 method for existing dwellings, which is often the deciding factor in whether a service upgrade is actually required.

When 220.87 is a good fit

  • An existing building undergoing renovation or electrification: a heat pump, EV charger, heat pump water heater, induction range.
  • A service upgrade is being suggested because of a calculated overload, not an observed one.
  • Utility AMI / smart-meter interval data is available for the property. (How to get it.)

It is not a fit for new construction, for a home with no usage history under its current occupants, or where a big load was added so recently that the last twelve months do not reflect it.

Recognized by regulators

"An acceptable means of calculating an existing service load." — Dennis Blair, Vermont Chief State Electrical Inspector

Acceptance still varies by jurisdiction, mostly because the method is unfamiliar rather than because it is disputed. What inspectors want to see in a 220.87 report covers how to make the submission easy to approve.
