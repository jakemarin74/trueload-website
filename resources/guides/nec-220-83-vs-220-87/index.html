NEC 220.83 vs. 220.87: Which Load Calculation for an Existing Home?

Both methods apply to existing dwellings and both are in the code. One models the house from nameplates, the other measures it. A worked example shows why the same 100-amp service can fail 220.83 and pass 220.87.

Updated Sep 21, 2026

When you add load to an existing home, the NEC gives you two practical ways to show the service can take it. Both are legitimate. They routinely give different answers. (Section numbers and the worked example follow the 2020 and 2023 editions. The 2026 edition moves both sections to Article 120, as 120.83 and 120.87, and changes the traditional method itself. See "Under the 2026 edition" below.)

  • 220.83 is the optional method for existing dwelling units. It builds the load up from square footage, circuits and appliance nameplates, then applies a demand factor. It applies to a dwelling unit on a 3-wire 120/240 V or 208Y/120 V service, with no minimum service size, so 60 A and 100 A services qualify.
  • 220.87 determines the existing load from measured maximum demand, takes it at 125 percent, and adds the new load.

How 220.83 works

You total the home's "other loads":

  • General lighting and receptacles at 3 VA per square foot
  • 1,500 VA for each small-appliance branch circuit and each laundry circuit
  • Every fastened-in-place appliance, range, dryer and water heater at nameplate

Then you apply the demand factor: the first 8 kVA at 100 percent and the remainder at 40 percent.

If the job adds air conditioning or electric space heating, which includes a heat pump, the calculation switches to 220.83(B): that equipment is taken at 100 percent on top, and only the larger connected load of air conditioning or space heating is used, not both.

How 220.87 works

You obtain twelve months of maximum demand data, find the highest demand, multiply by 1.25, and add the new load. If the total fits within the service rating, you are done. The full requirements are in our guide to NEC 220.87.

The same house, both ways

An 1,800 sq ft home with a 100-amp, 240-volt service. Electric range, dryer and water heater. The owner wants to replace an old central AC with a cold-climate heat pump: 7.2 kVA (30 A), no resistance backup.

220.83

Load VA
General lighting, 1,800 sq ft at 3 VA 5,400
Two small-appliance circuits + laundry 4,500
Range 12,000
Dryer 5,000
Water heater 4,500
Dishwasher 1,200
Disposal 900
Total other loads 33,500
First 8,000 at 100% 8,000
Remaining 25,500 at 40% 10,200
New heat pump at 100% 7,200
Calculated load 25,400 VA = 106 A

106 amps on a 100-amp service. On paper, this job needs a service upgrade.

220.87

Step
Measured 12-month peak from the utility meter 9.7 kW
At 125% 12.1 kW
New heat pump 7.2 kW
Calculated load 19.3 kW = 81 A

81 amps on a 100-amp service. The job goes ahead, with about 19 amps to spare. If that peak happened on a summer afternoon, it even includes the old AC that is being removed, so the real margin is larger. Under the 2026 edition you can subtract the removed AC outright.

The 9.7 kW figure is not cherry-picked: it is the mean measured peak across 11,940 homes in Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's research, which found 220.87 results roughly 40 percent below 220.83 on average. Any individual home can be higher or lower, which is the whole point of measuring it.

Under the 2026 edition

The 2026 NEC renumbers the measured-demand method as 120.87 and lets you subtract any load being removed, with the new load determined under Article 120's calculation rules. The traditional method changes more. 120.83 drops the (A)/(B) split and uses one table:

  • General lighting falls from 3 VA to 2 VA per square foot.
  • The first 8 kVA of existing and new load combined is taken at 100 percent, and the rest of the existing load at 40 percent.
  • New loads get their own percentages: new EV chargers at 80 percent, new central electric resistance heat at 80 percent, and all other new loads, including a heat pump, at 50 percent.

The same house under 120.83:

Load VA
General lighting, 1,800 sq ft at 2 VA 3,600
Small-appliance, laundry and appliances as above 28,100
Total existing load 31,700
First 8,000 at 100% 8,000
Remaining 23,700 existing at 40% 9,480
New heat pump at 50% 3,600
Calculated load 21,080 VA = 88 A

Under 2026 the traditional method passes this job too, where the 2023 version failed it. Check which edition your jurisdiction enforces before you pick a method. Massachusetts, for example, has enforced the 2026 NEC since April 2026.

When 220.83 is still the right tool

  • No interval data is available: no smart meter, a utility that will not release it, or new occupants with less than a year of history.
  • A major load was added within the last year and the data does not reflect it yet.
  • Solar or battery peak-shaving is masking the true load at the meter and you cannot account for it.
  • Your AHJ has told you they want it.

When to reach for 220.87

  • 220.83 comes out over the service rating and nobody has ever seen the main trip.
  • The customer is about to be quoted thousands for an upgrade on the strength of a worst-case estimate.
  • You want to qualify the job before rolling a truck. The existing-load half of 220.87 can be done entirely from the utility file.

In practice the two are not either/or. TrueLoad runs both from the same project: run the traditional calculation the day you open the job, and if it passes you are done. If it fails, request the customer's interval data and run measured demand in the same project, which is usually the lower number. When both have run, you pick which one the report is based on.
