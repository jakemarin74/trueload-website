How to Get AMI Interval Data From Your Utility

What to ask for, who has to ask for it, and how to tell whether the file you got back is actually usable.

Updated Aug 24, 2026

A 220.87 calculation is only as good as the meter data underneath it. This is the step where jobs stall — not because the data is hard to get, but because the request goes in wrong, or goes in from the wrong person.

Here is what actually works.

What you are asking for

Be specific. Vague requests get vague answers, and a well-meaning utility rep will often send a monthly billing summary instead, which is useless for this purpose.

Ask for:

  • Interval data — also called AMI data, smart meter data, or usage data. The word that matters is interval.
  • Twelve months — a full year, so the calculation qualifies under Condition (1).
  • The finest interval available — 15-minute if offered, hourly if not. Say so explicitly, because the default export is often the coarser one.
  • A machine-readable file — CSV, XML, or Green Button format. Not a PDF, and not a chart.

The word to avoid is "bill." Billing data is monthly totals. Monthly totals cannot show you a peak.

Who has to make the request

The customer of record does. Not you.

This is the single biggest source of delay in the whole process, and it is worth planning around rather than discovering. The utility's account holder is the homeowner or the building owner. You, the contractor, generally cannot pull their data — privacy rules exist precisely to stop that, and the utility will not make an exception because the job is on a deadline.

That gives you two paths:

Path one — the homeowner downloads it themselves. If the utility offers a self-service portal with Green Button "Download My Data", this is by far the cleanest route. No utility staff involved, no queue, often instant.

Path two — a signed authorization. The customer signs a release letting the utility send their data to you or your service provider. This adds days to weeks depending on the utility, but it works everywhere and does not depend on the homeowner's comfort with a web portal.

Assume the homeowner is the weakest link

Whichever path you take, design around this: the homeowner is the constraint, and login friction is where the process dies.

They have not logged into their utility account in three years. They do not know the password. The reset email goes to an address they abandoned. The account is in a spouse's name. The account is in a previous owner's name.

Every one of these is common. None of them is unusual enough to be worth being surprised by.

Practical things that help:

  • Send the request while you are standing in the house, not after you leave.
  • Send a link straight to the download page, not to the utility's homepage.
  • Tell them roughly how long it takes — "about five minutes, if you can get logged in" sets a truer expectation than "just grab your usage data."
  • Have the authorization path ready as a fallback before you need it. Switching to it after two weeks of silence has already cost you two weeks.

Green Button, briefly

Green Button is the industry-standard format for utility usage data, created in response to a 2012 federal call to action. Two flavors matter:

  • Download My Data (DMD) — the customer logs in and downloads a file. Customer-initiated, immediate, no integration required.
  • Connect My Data (CMD) — the customer authorizes ongoing automated delivery to a third party. Better long-term, much heavier to set up.

For a one-off permit calculation, DMD is what you want. Its main limitation is the one above: it requires the customer to successfully log in.

Checking the file you got back

Before you build anything on a dataset, look at four things.

1. Interval length. Look at consecutive timestamps. Fifteen minutes apart is ideal. Hourly is workable but must be converted, and the conversion has to be disclosed. Daily or monthly totals cannot be used for a demand calculation at all — send it back and ask again.

2. Coverage. Twelve months, with no long gaps. Meter changes, outages, and account transfers all leave holes. A gap across the coldest weeks of the year is the worst possible gap, because that is where the peak lives.

3. Units. Files arrive in kW or kWh and the two are not interchangeable. On 15-minute data, kWh must be multiplied by four to reach kW. Getting this backwards understates the peak by a factor of four — in the direction that wrongly tells you there is spare capacity.

4. Net versus gross, if there is solar. If the building has PV, ask whether the export is gross consumption or net of generation. Many standard exports are directional rather than gross. Net data understates real consumption, and building a capacity argument on an understated peak is exactly the failure mode you are trying to avoid.

When there is more than one meter

Some exports stack multiple meters into a single sheet — common where net metering is involved, and easy to miss because the file looks perfectly ordinary.

If two services are merged and treated as one, the resulting "peak" describes a panel that does not exist. Check the meter identifiers before you treat a file as a single service.

If the utility says no

It happens, particularly with smaller municipal utilities that have not built a self-service export.

Options, roughly in order:

  1. Ask for whatever interval data they do have, even if it is hourly and covers ten months. Something usable beats nothing.
  2. Ask specifically for the demand column if they bill demand — some utilities record maximum demand even when they do not publish full interval data.
  3. Fall back to a 30-day recording under the 220.87 Exception, which carries additional restrictions.
  4. Tell us which utility it was. Utilities respond to their own customers far better than to vendors — if several contractors in a territory are asking for the same thing, that is a conversation worth having, and it is one we will have on your behalf.
