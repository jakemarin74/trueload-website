This is a screening estimate, not a load calculation for a permit. A real NEC 220.87 calculation uses the home's own 12 months of interval data, the nameplate of the equipment being installed, and a judgment about which loads run at the same time as the measured peak. It must be prepared by a licensed professional, and the local inspector has the final say.

The 9.7 kW default is the average measured peak across 11,940 homes studied by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. An individual home can be well above or below it. Treats kW as kVA, which is close for most homes.

How the calculation works

NEC 220.87 lets the load on an existing service be based on measured maximum demand instead of a worst-case estimate built from appliance nameplates. The highest demand recorded over a year is multiplied by 125 percent, the new load is added, and the total must not exceed the rating of the service.

To convert: amps = kW × 1,000 ÷ 240. A 100-amp service is 24 kW. A 9.7 kW peak is about 40 amps, or 50 amps once the 125 percent factor is applied, which leaves roughly 50 amps for new equipment.

For comparison with the traditional approach, see 220.83 vs. 220.87. To find a home's actual peak, see how to get interval data from your utility.
